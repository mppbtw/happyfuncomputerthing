Overview

The internet revolutionised the sharing of media. Think about Netflix, Youtube, Twitch, Spotify e.t.c. How would you ever live without these? The global video streaming industry has grown significantly, with millions of people subscribing to services such as Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, HBO Max, and YouTube Premium. Based on publicly reported figures, the combined number of subscriptions, memberships, and estimated audiences across major streaming platforms exceeds 1.2 billion. Netflix alone has approximately 325 million subscribers, while Amazon Prime has over 200 million members. However, these figures do not represent 1.2 billion unique individuals, as many people subscribe to multiple platforms and some accounts are shared among several viewers. Despite this overlap, the figures demonstrate the enormous global demand for streaming entertainment and the significant market opportunity for existing and emerging streaming services..

The first streaming services

The earliest notable internet streaming software was RealNetworks' RealPlayer, launched in 1995 to stream audio and video files. For live internet broadcasting, Mark Cuban and Todd Wagner launched Audionet (later known as Broadcast.com) in 1994, which streamed live sports games and radio over the web.

So... how do they work?

  • Source & Encoding: Video files are stored on large servers and compressed into various quality levels (such as 720p, 1080p, or 4K) using different video formats (codecs).
  • Content Delivery Networks (CDNs): Instead of every user connecting to one central computer, services use global networks of distributed servers called CDNs. When you press play, data comes from the server physically closest to you.
  • Chunking & Buffering: The video is sent in tiny segments (often a fraction of a second long). Your device plays them in real-time as they arrive.
  • Adaptive Bitrate: The service automatically checks your internet speed. If your Wi-Fi slows down, it temporarily lowers the video resolution (buffers or drops quality) so the show keeps playing without stopping.
  • Digital Rights Management (DRM): Built-in software apps prevent you from easily recording or permanently downloading and saving the raw media files to your device.

The impact

United Kingdom: Approximately 20.1 to 20.8 million households (about 67% to 70%) subscribe to a paid video streaming service. Netflix is the most popular platform, used by roughly 58% to 61% of UK homes

United States: About 91% of households pay for video streaming services, with the average user maintaining roughly 2.8 to 4 active subscriptions.

Other impacts of computing on entertainment

  • Personalised recommendations: Algorithms analyse viewing and listening habits to suggest films, programmes, music, and games that users may enjoy.
  • Interactive experiences: Computer graphics, virtual reality, and augmented reality make entertainment more immersive and allow audiences to influence what happens.
  • Content creation: Video-editing software, digital cameras, music-production programs, and animation tools allow individuals to create high-quality content without a large studio.
  • Opportunities for creators: Musicians, filmmakers, streamers, and game developers can publish directly to a global audience and earn money through advertising, subscriptions, and donations.
  • Accessibility: Captions, audio descriptions, screen readers, adjustable playback speeds, and accessible controllers help more people enjoy entertainment.
  • Copyright challenges: Digital copying makes content easy to share, but it also increases piracy and raises questions about ownership and fair payment for creators.
  • Social connection: Multiplayer games, livestream chats, and online fan communities allow people to share entertainment and communicate across the world.
  • Environmental impact: Data centres, networks, and electronic devices use energy and raw materials, contributing to carbon emissions and electronic waste.
